Introduction to Global South

What does Global South mean ?

The Global South is a term describing a broad grouping of countries, primarily from Africa, Asia, Latin America, and Oceania, that share a sense of underrepresentation in international systems. It is not a formal bloc nor strictly geographical, as it includes countries like China and India. Key groupings such as the G77 and the Non-Aligned Movement represent these nations as they assert themselves against a world order they believe was designed by and for Western powers. They argue that multilateral institutions like the UN, World Bank, and IMF benefit "only the selected few" and fail to adequately represent their interests.

These countries often bear a disproportionate burden from global issues—such as climate change, pandemics, and economic crises—that they did not create. Consequently, many are resisting pressure to align with major powers like the US, China, or Russia. However, the term "Global South" itself is contentious. Critics argue it is an overly simplistic label that masks the vast diversity among its members, from small island states to major economies, making unified action difficult and potentially creating a "barrier to understanding a complex world."

Women Artists from the Global South

Triple Marginalization

Women artists from the Global South face compounding struggles that go beyond what any single label can hold. Media from the Global North frequently portrays them through narrow, stereotypical lenses, exoticised figures, passive victims, denying their individuality and making it difficult for their authentic voices to be heard.The words “female,” “identities,” and “Global South” thus emerge as imposed labels that bring about what Goniwe calls the “burden of racial representation” an imposed identity that erases agency.

Their struggle is distinctly gendered: on top of economic and political hardships shared with men in their regions, women face additional systemic barriers — disproportionate unpaid care burdens, legal inequities, and limited access to resources — that compound into a cycle of constrained opportunity. Ultimately, supporting their voice and leadership is fundamental to any genuine understanding of global art.

Representation in Institutions

Representation through data

Our data from Videomuseum — a network of French museums and institutions managing collections of modern and contemporary art, including national, regional, departmental, municipal museums, Cnap, Frac, and foundations — documents 3485 artists.
Women artists make up less than 1 in 4 of all recorded artists. Women from the Global South represent only 1 in 10 artists total, and 44% of all women artists (343 out of 782).

Featured Artists : Marina Abramović

Born into a household shaped by war and ideology she left at 29 and "literally escaped."

Marina Abramović was born in 1946 in Belgrade, into a family marked by sacrifice. Both parents were Yugoslav Partisans who fought alongside Tito; her father became a general. Her upbringing was strict, militaristic, and governed by an iron curfew her mother enforced until she was 29. All her early performances in Yugoslavia had to be finished before ten in the evening.

In 1976, she left for Amsterdam not as a career move, but as a break for freedom. As she put it, she "literally escaped," and her mother went to the police to report her disappearance. It was the beginning of a nomadic life she has described ever since as liberation: "I see myself as a modern nomad. The entire planet is my studio."

Context & inspiration

What Abramović brought to the West was not simply technique but an entire worldview forged in Yugoslavia — a country of endurance, sacrifice, and collective memory of trauma. Her Balkan origins are present in nearly every major work: in the ritualism, the confrontation with pain, the refusal of comfort.Balkan Baroque (Venice Biennale, 1997) made this explicit. For four days, she scrubbed 1,500 bloody cow bones while singing folk songs from her childhood, a direct response to the violent images of the Yugoslav Wars. The work, which won the Golden Lion, was described by critics as a form of spiritual purification: a belief in art's power to redeem what politics had destroyed.

She has acknowledged that the country's history of conflict, its complex religious heritage, she spent her early childhood in the Serbian Orthodox Church with her grandmother and its rituals of mourning left an indelible mark. "This work is very personal, but it also speaks so much about that Yugoslavia which no longer exists."

Featured Artists : Maria Tereza Alves

Her family was forced to leave Brazil. Displacement became the lens through which she would examine all of history.

Maria Thereza Alves was born in São Paulo in 1961. When she was still a child, her family left Brazil for New York not by choice, but out of necessity: they were fleeing the military dictatorship that had seized power in 1964. That first forced rupture between a person and the place they were born would become the defining question of her entire practice.

In New York she studied architecture at Cooper Union (BFA, 1985). But her path moved quickly beyond buildings. As a teenager, she presented to the United Nations Human Rights Committee in Geneva on indigenous rights abuses in Brazil. In 1987 she co-founded Brazil's Green Party. For Alves, art, ecology, and politics were never separate disciplines.

Process and themes

Her long-term project Seeds of Change, begun in 1999, investigates the ballast flora deposited in port cities during the colonial period. Colonial ships used soil, stones, and organic matter ( including seeds ) to stabilise their vessels. When they arrived in new ports, the ballast was discarded. The seeds took root, growing quietly in the margins of cities built on the trade routes that carried them. Alves traced these seeds to Marseille, Liverpool, Bristol, Antwerp, Dunkirk, Reposaari, and New York, uncovering in each city a different chapter of colonial history still living in the soil. The project was awarded the Jane Lombard Prize for Art and Social Justice by the Vera List Center at The New School.

Her approach deliberately resists Western binaries — art and politics, nature and culture. She works with communities as equals, through processes of dialogue and exchange, understanding material realities and social histories as deeply interconnected rather than separate domains.

Interview new artists : Leticia Pantoja

A new voice in contemporary art

Leticia Pantoja's story sits in deliberate contrast to the others in this collection. Where Abramović left Yugoslavia at 29 and "literally escaped," and where Alves's family was forced from Brazil by dictatorship, Pantoja chose to stay. Her international reputation was built from within Brazil, not by seeking validation abroad, but by building, performing, and creating infrastructure at home.

VJ since 2007, the visual artist Leticia Pantoja is one of the pioneering women in the profession of Video Jockey in Brazil. During these 15 years of career, she has traveled to several art and technology festivals in Brazil and around the world, such as Amazônia Mapping, Mostra Museu on Paulista Avenue and even Christ the Redeemer, she has already covered with her images. He has presentations with artists such as Anitta, Elza Soares, Alceu Valença, Fernanda Abreu, Otto, Marina Sena, among others.

Leticia Pantoja has worked with a variety of artists and events, including major festivals such as Rock in Rio and Lollapalooza. She is also the creator of the festival Ventre mapping, which focuses on promoting and developing the female video art scene in Brazil. It combines different techniques, such as live cinema, video mapping and interactive installations, to create impressive visual experiences that blend with music, digital collage, poetry and dance.

Her presence in this collection represents a different kind of urgency: not the urgency of exile, but the urgency of building what doesn't yet exist and insisting that it be recognised where it already is.
